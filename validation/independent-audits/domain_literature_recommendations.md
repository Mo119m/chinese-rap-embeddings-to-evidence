# Domain-literature recommendations for the Chinese-rap paper

Audit date: 25 August 2026  
Target: *Digital Scholarship in the Humanities*  
Scope: Chinese rap lyrical repertoires, cultural references, and dictionary-estimated written rhyme

## Recommendation in one paragraph

The paper should not treat Chinese rap as a generic multilingual text collection. A compact domain strand can establish a more defensible argument: Chinese rap language is a situated repertoire through which performers localize a global genre, index places and social types, negotiate authenticity, and manipulate phonological material in performance. That literature supports the project's term **lyrical repertoire**, but it also tightens the claim boundary. A source-label profile is not a stable person, a place mention is not a hometown, and a pinyin-derived ending class is not performed rhyme or flow. The eight sources below are sufficient; adding broad hip-hop surveys on top of them would be ornamental unless a specific historical claim is introduced.

## Coverage map

| Strand | Core sources | Direct use in this paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early localization and cultural history | Barrett (2012); Liu (2014) | Frame Chinese rap as locally reworked linguistic practice rather than an imitation of an English-language genre. |
| Dialect, code-mixing, and authenticity | Wang (2013); Liu (2021) | Justify repertoire-level interpretation while warning that automated language features cannot by themselves establish authenticity or identity. |
| Named places, multilingual reference, and persona | Baioud (2024) | Give RQ2 a domain-grounded reason to retain place/reference edges while refusing hometown or social-relation claims. |
| Rhyming style and persona | Lin and Wang (2024) | Show that rhyme can participate in persona construction, but only in a broader multimodal and contextual analysis. |
| Imperfect/crosslinguistic rhyme | Wang and Lin (2024) | Establish what a fixed dictionary-family system misses: performed repair, regional variation, and Chinese–English imperfect rhyme. |
| Tone, line endings, and performed flow | Liu *et al.* (2023) | Supply the decisive audio-based boundary for RQ3: written pinyin endings cannot measure tonal realization, cadence, beat alignment, or flow. |

## Eight sources to integrate

### 1. Barrett (2012) — local identity is interculturally made

**Verified citation**  
Barrett, C. (2012). Hip-Hopping Across China: Intercultural Formulations of Local Identities. *Journal of Language, Identity & Education*, 11(4): 247–260. https://doi.org/10.1080/15348458.2012.706172

The article combines interviews, lyric analysis, studio observation, and live-performance observation to show that global hip-hop resources are recruited and reinterpreted for locally significant practices in China. It directly supports the paper's RQ1/RQ4 framing of a *lyrical repertoire* as a mixture of linguistic and cultural signals rather than a pure, context-free style vector.

**Claim boundary:** it does not justify equating a corpus source label with a coherent real-world identity; the study's identities are interactionally and interculturally produced.

**Best placement:** Related Work, immediately before or after the definition of *lyrical repertoire*.

Official publisher record: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15348458.2012.706172

### 2. Wang (2013) — dialect mixing requires contextualization

**Verified citation**  
Wang, X. (2013). “I am not a qualified dialect rapper”: constructing hip-hop authenticity in China. *Sociolinguistic Studies*, 6(2): 333–372. https://doi.org/10.1558/sols.v6i2.333

Wang analyzes the mixing of a stigmatized local variety, Putonghua, and English in rural Chinese hip-hop and argues that the combination becomes intelligible only through ethnographic contextualization and multiple “orders of authenticity.” This is a direct warning for RQ1/RQ4: proportions of dialectal, standard, or English material can describe a corpus repertoire, but they cannot independently measure authenticity.

**Claim boundary:** never turn code choice, dialect markers, or embedding similarity into “how authentic this rapper is.”

**Best placement:** Related Work subsection on repertoire versus identity; reuse in Limitations when describing the absence of ethnographic evidence.

Official DOI metadata resolves to the University of Toronto Press publisher record: https://doi.org/10.1558/sols.v6i2.333

### 3. Liu (2014) — local language and place are substantive lyrical resources

**Verified citation**  
Liu, J. (2014). Alternative Voice and Local Youth Identity in Chinese Local-Language Rap Music. *positions: asia critique*, 22(1): 263–292. https://doi.org/10.1215/10679847-2383840

Through close reading of local-language rap texts and fieldwork interviews, Liu shows that regional language, slang, local knowledge, and representations of city/region can construct a musicalized local identity and carry social commentary. This directly motivates RQ2's attention to places and other cultural references, and it helps explain why character-form evidence can complement semantic embeddings in RQ1.

**Claim boundary:** the study supports interpreting place names as resources of lyrical localization; an extracted place mention alone still does not establish residence, origin, membership, or affiliation.

**Best placement:** Related Work at the transition from language repertoire to cultural-reference extraction; Discussion of the six supported label–place associations.

Official author/institution record: https://modlangs.gatech.edu/publications/pub/5062  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1215/10679847-2383840

### 4. Liu (2021) — script, language choice, and vocal intelligibility are different layers

**Verified citation**  
Liu, J. (2021). Language, identity and unintelligibility: A case study of the rap group Higher Brothers. *East Asian Journal of Popular Culture*, 7(1): 43–59. https://doi.org/10.1386/eapc_00038_1

This case study analyzes Chengdu Mandarin, Putonghua, and English in Higher Brothers' songs before and after international mediation, while treating vocal unintelligibility as part of trap's sonic aesthetics. It directly informs RQ1/RQ4 by showing why multilingual written material can retrieve a repertoire, yet lyrics alone do not capture the vocal production through which local, national, and global identities are negotiated.

**Claim boundary:** a transcript-based model must not infer performed intelligibility, accent, mumble-rap delivery, or global/local identity from text alone.

**Best placement:** Related Work on multilingual repertoire and in the paper's audio/performance limitation.

Official publisher record: https://intellectdiscover.com/content/journals/10.1386/eapc_00038_1

### 5. Baioud (2024) — place references can localize a persona without proving biography

**Verified citation**  
Baioud, G. (2024). Constructing ‘corrupted village wives and urban men’ through multilingual performances. *Language in Society*, 53(1): 25–45. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0047404522000665

Baioud's study of multilingual performance in Inner Mongolia shows how Mandarin, Mongolian, Jin dialect, consumer objects, a self-reference, a hometown name, and Hohhot landmarks jointly build an ironic urban persona. It is unusually useful for RQ2 because it demonstrates the cultural meaning of named references while also showing that their meaning depends on stance, stylization, and performance context.

**Claim boundary:** a label–place edge means that a label's retained lyric corpus references a place more than expected under the stated statistical model; it is not evidence of hometown, residence, cultural membership, friendship, or collaboration.

**Best placement:** Related Work on cultural-reference networks and the RQ2 Discussion paragraph explaining the six supported label–place edges.

Official publisher record: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-in-society/article/constructing-corrupted-village-wives-and-urban-men-through-multilingual-performances/0EF7DDDAFF07DC8CB2D855965A938626

### 6. Lin and Wang (2024) — rhyme participates in persona, but not by itself

**Verified citation**  
Lin, Y. and Wang, T. (2024). Rhyming style, persona, and the contested landscape of authentic Chinese hip hop. *Journal of Sociolinguistics*, 28(2): 22–41. https://doi.org/10.1111/josl.12635

The study quantitatively describes rhyming styles in *The Rap of China* and then identifies rapper personae through the joint analysis of language use, visual representation, lyrical themes, performance, and audience reaction. It gives RQ3/RQ4 a direct domain precedent for treating rhyme as one component of a larger semiotic repertoire.

**Claim boundary:** this paper's 17-class ending-family predictor cannot infer persona or authenticity because it lacks the multimodal and interactional evidence used by Lin and Wang.

**Best placement:** Related Work subsection on rhyme; Discussion of why the present source-label ablation does not support rapper-specific prediction.

Official publisher record: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/josl.12635

### 7. Wang and Lin (2024) — imperfect rhyme exposes the limit of fixed pinyin classes

**Verified citation**  
Wang, T. and Lin, Y. (2024). Variation is the way to perfection: imperfect rhyming in Chinese hip hop. *Linguistics Vanguard*, 10(1): 505–515. https://doi.org/10.1515/lingvan-2024-0093

Using 369 monolingual and crosslinguistic imperfect-rhyme instances from a Chinese rap competition, the authors show how performers increase perceived rhymability through traditional Chinese schemes, rock-rhyme patterns, Southern Mandarin variation, and Chinese-accented English. This source directly defines what RQ3's dictionary normalization excludes and suggests a high-value future extension based on audio-aligned rhyme realization.

**Claim boundary:** do not label same-family pinyin endings “true rhymes,” and do not treat different-family endings as non-rhymes; both judgments can be changed by regional and performed realization.

**Best placement:** Rhyme Related Work and the first paragraph of Rhyme Limitations.

Official publisher record: https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/lingvan-2024-0093/html

### 8. Liu *et al.* (2023) — audio evidence makes the written-rhyme boundary non-negotiable

**Verified citation**  
Liu, J., Dong, H., Yuan, J., Ma, H. and She, A. (2023). Linguistic tone in Chinese rap: an interdisciplinary approach. *Journal of New Music Research*, 52(4): 265–284. https://doi.org/10.1080/09298215.2024.2329075

The authors analyze more than 8,000 syllables, 140 Mandarin rap songs, and interviews with five rappers using an audio-based Tonal Congruence Index; they report subgenre differences in tone preservation, a high share of fourth-tone line endings, and increasing use of English for flow. This is the strongest direct support for the RQ3 boundary: pinyin-derived written endings are analytically useful, but they cannot measure performed tone, rhythm, cadence, beat alignment, or flow.

**Claim boundary:** the present study may compare dictionary-estimated written-ending families, but any statement about tonal preservation, trap/boom-bap performance, or English facilitating flow requires aligned audio.

**Best placement:** final paragraph of Rhyme Related Work and repeated once in Rhyme Limitations.

Official publisher record: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09298215.2024.2329075

## Bibliographic-year notes

- Liu *et al.* is assigned to volume 52, issue 4 (2023), pages 265–284, although the version of record was posted online on 29 March 2024. Cite **2023** when using the issue year, and keep that choice consistent in text and references.
- Lin and Wang is assigned to the April 2024 issue of volume 28, although its version of record appeared online in October 2023. Cite **2024**.
- Baioud is assigned to the February 2024 issue of volume 53, although it appeared online in January 2023. Cite **2024**.
- Crossref's publisher-deposited metadata dates Wang's *Sociolinguistic Studies* article to 29 May 2013. Some secondary bibliographies label the volume 2012; use **2013** unless the authors choose to follow the year printed on an accessed version of record, then normalize both in-text and reference-list dates together.

## Minimal integration plan

1. Add one concise paragraph to Related Work that combines Barrett (2012), Wang (2013), Liu (2014), and Liu (2021): repertoire is local, mixed, and performed; it is not a pure style or stable identity.
2. Add Baioud (2024) to the RQ2 motivation and interpretation: named places are meaningful localization resources, while place edges remain textual associations only.
3. Replace the generic rhyme boundary with a three-source chain: Lin and Wang (2024) for rhyme-plus-persona, Wang and Lin (2024) for imperfect/performed rhyme, and Liu *et al.* (2023) for tone and audio.
4. Do not add claims about censorship, scene chronology, gender, race, or audience reception unless the manuscript develops and tests those questions. The present corpus cannot support them merely because the literature discusses them.

## Verification policy

Titles, authors, volume/issue, pages, DOI, and publisher were reconciled against publisher pages and publisher-deposited Crossref records. The relevance notes above paraphrase publisher abstracts or full publisher descriptions; no unverified source was retained.
